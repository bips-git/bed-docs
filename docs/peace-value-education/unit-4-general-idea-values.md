## Classification of Values: Personal and Social

Values are broad principles that guide human behavior. To make sense of them, educators organize values into a nested classification structure based on different criteria:

*   **1. Top-Level Split (Scope):** Personal vs. Social Values
*   **2. Classification by Motivation:** Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Values
*   **3. Classification by Origin:** Social/Democratic vs. Moral/Spiritual Values

The top-level split divides all values based on their scope. **Personal values** are principles that guide an individual's own life, character, and self-development, such as ambition, cleanliness, or self-discipline. **Social values** govern how that individual interacts with others within a community, emphasizing harmony, such as courtesy, teamwork, and tolerance.

::: tip Example
A student making their bed every morning before school is demonstrating a **personal value** (cleanliness). That same student helping a classmate pick up dropped books in the hallway is demonstrating a **social value** (helpfulness).
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher: Recognizing this split helps a teacher understand whether a student needs help managing their own internal habits or needs guidance on interacting with their peers.*

## Intrinsic and Extrinsic Values

The second classification basis looks at the motivation behind a value—specifically, whether it is driven by personal interest or a broader social good. 

**Intrinsic values** are driven by personal interest; they are valued entirely for their own sake and the internal satisfaction they bring. **Extrinsic values** are driven by a desire to achieve a practical social good or an external reward; they act as a means to an end.

::: tip Example
A student playing the violin purely because they love the sound of music holds an **intrinsic value**. If the student plays the violin strictly to win a talent show and earn money for their school's charity fund, they are holding an **extrinsic value**.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher: By identifying whether a student is intrinsically or extrinsically motivated, a teacher can tailor assignments to either spark their inner passion or offer clear, tangible rewards.*

## Social, Moral, Spiritual and Democratic Values

The third classification basis categorizes values by their origin: whether they are externally imposed by society or internally generated.

**Social and Democratic values** are based on *societal expectation*. They are the rules required to keep a nation functioning fairly, such as justice, secularism, equality, and civic duty. Conversely, **Moral and Spiritual values** are based on *self-inspiration*. They arise from an individual's deep inner conscience or religious awakening, focusing on ultimate truths, inner peace, and unconditional compassion, regardless of what the law demands.

::: tip Example
Waiting in a queue at the cafeteria because it is the fair rule of the school is a **democratic value** based on societal expectation. Forgiving a classmate who accidentally spilled water on you, even when you have the right to be angry, is a **spiritual value** based on self-inspiration.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher: This distinction helps a teacher know when to enforce strict classroom rules (democratic values) and when to appeal to a student's deeper sense of empathy and conscience (moral values).*

## Identification and Analysis of Value Conflicts

A value conflict occurs when an individual faces a situation where two or more of their core values directly contradict each other. Upholding one value inevitably means violating the other. Identification involves recognizing the competing moral demands in a situation. Analysis involves weighing the consequences of each choice and deciding which value serves the greater ethical good.

::: tip Example
A student sees their best friend cheating on an exam. The student faces a direct value conflict between **honesty** (telling the teacher) and **loyalty** (protecting the friend). A teacher helps analyze this conflict by guiding the student to see that ignoring the cheating ultimately harms the friend's long-term learning.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher: Teachers who understand value conflicts will not immediately punish a hesitating student, recognizing instead that the child might be paralyzed by a difficult moral dilemma rather than just being defiant.*

## Designing and Developing Instructional Material for Nurturing Values

Instructional material for value education cannot simply be a list of rules to memorize. Designing and developing these materials requires a deliberate focus on storytelling, emotional engagement, and real-world relevance. The development process typically starts with selecting a specific target value, researching age-appropriate scenarios, and creating content that forces students to think critically. This often involves writing case studies, scripting role-play exercises, or selecting historical biographies that highlight moral courage.

::: tip Example
To nurture the value of cooperation, a teacher designs a group-work puzzle rather than handing out a standard worksheet. The instructional material is developed so that the puzzle physically cannot be completed unless every student shares their specific pieces, forcing them to practice cooperation rather than just reading about it.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher: Creating well-designed materials ensures that value education is an active, emotionally engaging experience rather than a passive, ineffective lecture.*

## Characteristics of Instructional Material for Values

While the previous section covers how materials are made, this section covers what makes them effective. Good instructional material for nurturing values must possess specific characteristics. It must be **relatable**, featuring characters and situations that mirror the students' actual daily lives. It must be **unbiased and secular**, presenting moral dilemmas without favoring one specific religion. It should be **open-ended**, encouraging discussion rather than forcing a single rigid answer. Finally, it must be **age-appropriate**, matching the cognitive and emotional maturity of the classroom.

::: tip Example
An effective, relatable instructional story for 8-year-olds about "fairness" features a scenario about sharing playground swings. An ineffective story for that age group would try to explain "fairness" using complex, unrelatable examples about adult tax laws.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher: Evaluating materials against these characteristics ensures a teacher does not waste class time using resources that are too preachy, culturally insensitive, or developmentally inappropriate.*

## Key Terms

- **Intrinsic Value** — A principle or activity that is valued purely for its own sake and the internal joy it brings.
- **Extrinsic Value** — A principle or activity that is valued because it acts as a means to achieve an external goal or social benefit.
- **Value Conflict** — A difficult situation where two or more core moral beliefs contradict each other, requiring a tough ethical choice.
- **Instructional Material** — Any resource, such as a story, puzzle, or case study, used by a teacher to facilitate learning.

## Quick Recap

- Values are classified in a nested structure based on their scope, motivation, and origin.
- Personal values guide self-development, while social values guide community interaction.
- Intrinsic values are driven by personal interest, whereas extrinsic values serve a broader social good.
- Democratic values stem from societal expectations, while spiritual values arise from inner self-inspiration.
- Value conflicts, such as choosing between honesty and loyalty, require careful identification and empathetic analysis.
- Designing value education materials requires creating active, scenario-based content like role-plays and case studies.
- Effective instructional materials are relatable, unbiased, age-appropriate, and encourage open-ended discussion.
