## Meaning and Concept of Normality and Abnormality

Normality and abnormality are not simple, black-and-white categories. Instead, they exist on a spectrum. Psychologists and scholars often disagree on where exactly to draw the line between normal and abnormal behavior, so they use a combination of different perspectives to define it. 

The **statistical perspective** suggests that behavior is abnormal if it is mathematically rare (though this is flawed, as extreme intelligence is rare but not a mental illness). The **social norm perspective** defines abnormality as behavior that drastically violates cultural rules. Finally, the **functional impairment perspective**—which is the most widely accepted in psychology—defines abnormality as behavior that causes severe personal distress or prevents a person from performing daily tasks, maintaining relationships, or living safely. 

::: tip Example
If a Class 6 student insists on wearing winter gloves in the summer, they might just be violating a social norm (acting uniquely). However, if they have an intense, paralyzing fear of touching their desk that prevents them from writing or participating in class, this is a functional impairment and leans toward abnormal behavior.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher:* Teachers must realize that just because a student acts differently or uniquely (breaking a social norm) doesn't automatically mean they have a mental illness (functional abnormality).

## Causal Factors of Abnormal Behaviour: Biological and Psychological

Mental illness is rarely caused by just one thing. It is usually a complex mix of biological and psychological factors. 

**Biological Factors**
These are physical issues within the body and brain that lead to abnormal behavior. Key biological factors include:
- **Genetics:** Inheriting a vulnerability to certain mental illnesses from parents.
- **Neurochemical imbalances:** An oversupply or undersupply of neurotransmitters (chemical messengers in the brain), like serotonin or dopamine.
- **Brain dysfunction or injury:** Physical damage to the brain from accidents, infections, or prenatal complications.

::: tip Example
A student with a strong family history of severe mood swings might have a genetic vulnerability, making them biologically more susceptible to developing depression during the stressful teenage years.
:::

**Psychological Factors**
These relate to the mind, life experiences, and how a person processes the world. Key psychological factors include:
- **Early childhood trauma:** Experiences of severe neglect, abuse, or loss during early developmental years.
- **Chronic stress:** Prolonged exposure to hostile environments, such as severe poverty or intense domestic conflict.
- **Maladaptive learning:** Learning unhealthy coping mechanisms or deep-seated fears by watching parents or caregivers react negatively to the world.

::: tip Example
A student who witnessed severe domestic violence at home (childhood trauma) might develop extreme, disruptive anxiety in the classroom, driven entirely by psychological stress rather than a genetic brain issue.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher:* Understanding these causes helps teachers view abnormal behavior not as a "bad choice" made by a disobedient student, but as a complex result of biology and life history requiring professional empathy and support.

## Classification of Mental Illness (DSM-IV)

To properly study and treat mental illnesses, professionals need a shared language to organize them. The official syllabus for this course references the **DSM-IV** (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition). *(Note: In modern clinical practice, the DSM-IV has since been superseded by the updated DSM-5 and DSM-5-TR, though the foundational categories remain similar).*

The DSM-IV organized mental illnesses into broad categories based on shared behavioral and psychological symptoms. For a teacher's general awareness, some of the major broad groupings include:
- **Disorders Usually First Diagnosed in Infancy, Childhood, or Adolescence:** Conditions that appear early in life, such as Learning Disorders, Autism, and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD).
- **Anxiety Disorders:** Conditions characterized by overwhelming, irrational fear and worry, such as Phobias and Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD).
- **Mood Disorders:** Severe disturbances in emotion, such as Major Depressive Disorder and Bipolar Disorder.
- **Schizophrenia and Other Psychotic Disorders:** Severe conditions where a person loses touch with reality, experiencing hallucinations or deep delusions.
- **Eating Disorders:** Severe disturbances in eating behavior, such as Anorexia or Bulimia.

::: tip Example
If a school psychologist informs a teacher that a student has been diagnosed with ADHD, the teacher understands from the classification system that this falls under early childhood developmental disorders, indicating a recognized medical condition that requires a modified teaching approach.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher:* Being familiar with these broad categories allows teachers to understand the basic terminology used by school psychologists and medical professionals in a student's file, without needing to be diagnostic experts themselves.

## Key Terms

- **Normality** — Behavior that allows a person to function safely, adapt to their environment, and meet the daily demands of life.
- **Abnormality** — Behavior that is atypical, causes significant personal distress, and severely impairs a person's ability to function normally.
- **Neurotransmitter** — Chemical messengers in the brain that, when unbalanced, can act as a biological cause for mental illness.
- **DSM** — The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, a standard guidebook used globally by professionals to classify mental illnesses.

## Quick Recap

- Normality and abnormality exist on a spectrum and are judged by statistical frequency, cultural norms, and most importantly, functional impairment.
- Biological causes of abnormal behavior include genetics, brain injury, and chemical imbalances in the brain.
- Psychological causes of abnormal behavior include childhood trauma, severe stress, and unhealthy learned behaviors.
- The DSM-IV (now updated to DSM-5) classifies mental illnesses into broad categories like mood, anxiety, and childhood developmental disorders to help professionals communicate clearly.
