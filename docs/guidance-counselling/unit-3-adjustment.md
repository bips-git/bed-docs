# Unit III. Adjustment and Maladjustment

## Good Adjustment: Concept, Purpose, Techniques and Criteria

Adjustment is the continuous process by which an individual maintains a harmonious balance between their own personal needs and the demands of their environment. The purpose of good adjustment is to allow a person to live a peaceful, productive life free from severe psychological tension or conflict. 

To achieve this, individuals use various techniques. Direct techniques involve consciously tackling the problem, such as working harder after a failure or compromising. Indirect techniques involve unconscious "defense mechanisms," such as rationalization (making excuses for a failure) or sublimation (channeling aggressive energy into sports). The criteria for good adjustment include emotional maturity, a realistic self-perception, the ability to form healthy social relationships, and the capacity to meet daily responsibilities without breaking down.

::: tip Example
A rural student transitioning to a city-based English-medium school faces a severe language barrier. If the student acknowledges this weakness realistically and joins an after-school language study group (a direct problem-solving technique) rather than quitting, they are demonstrating good adjustment.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher:* Recognizing the techniques of good adjustment allows a teacher to praise and reinforce positive coping mechanisms in students, rather than just focusing on academic scores.

## Maladjustment: Causes, Prevention and Remedies

Maladjustment occurs when an individual fails to maintain a healthy balance between their needs and environmental demands, leading to chronic frustration, unhappiness, and behavioral issues. 

The causes of maladjustment broadly fall into three categories. Personal causes include physical disabilities, chronic illness, or emotional instability. Environmental causes often stem from the home, such as broken families, extreme poverty, or overly strict parenting. School-related causes involve harsh discipline, bullying by peers, an overly rigid curriculum, or a lack of sympathetic teachers. 

Prevention is always better than cure. Schools can prevent maladjustment by maintaining a democratic, inclusive environment, offering co-curricular activities to release pent-up energy, and ensuring the basic emotional needs of students are met. When maladjustment occurs, remedies involve the teacher identifying the root cause rather than just punishing the symptom. The teacher must collaborate with parents, modify their own teaching approach to be more supportive, and refer the student to a guidance counselor for targeted help.

::: tip Example
A Class 8 student constantly disrupts the class and picks fights. Instead of simply suspending him, a teacher speaks to the parents and discovers the child is facing severe neglect at home. The school then provides a supportive mentor and involves him in the school football team to channel his energy constructively.
:::

*Why this matters for a teacher:* Understanding that bad behavior is usually a symptom of a deeper, unfulfilled need helps teachers shift from a punitive mindset to a supportive, problem-solving mindset.

## Common Maladjusted Behaviours a Teacher Should Recognize

| Behaviour | What a Teacher Might Observe | Teacher's Response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Truancy** | A student frequently skipping school or specific classes without permission or a valid medical reason. | Document absences, contact parents to understand home circumstances, and make the classroom more engaging. |
| **Lying** | Habitual falsehoods used to avoid punishment, gain attention, or manipulate peers, even when the truth is obvious. | Avoid public humiliation; privately discuss the root fear causing the lying and reward honesty. |
| **Timidity** | Extreme shyness, avoiding eye contact, refusing to participate in group activities, and fear of speaking up. | Provide small, low-pressure responsibilities to build confidence gradually without forcing sudden public exposure. |
| **Stealing** | Taking items from classmates' bags or school property without permission, often stemming from deprivation or a need for attention. | Address the behavior privately without branding the child a "thief," find out if basic needs are unmet, and teach respect for property. |
| **Anxiety** | Constant visible worry, nail-biting, physical restlessness, or sweating, especially before exams or speaking tasks. | Create a calm classroom atmosphere, teach basic deep-breathing exercises, and avoid hyper-competitive grading pressure. |
| **Phobia** | An irrational, intense fear of a specific situation (like speaking on stage) or object that causes the student to freeze or flee. | Never force the student directly into the feared situation; use gentle, gradual exposure and consult a school counselor. |
| **Hysteria** | Dramatic emotional crying fits, sudden unexplained fainting, or physical complaints (like stomach aches) immediately prior to stressful events. | Remain calm, remove the student from an audience to reduce panic, ensure physical safety, and refer to a medical/counseling professional. |
| **OCD** | Repeatedly washing hands, checking bags multiple times, or rigidly arranging desk items, causing the student severe distress if interrupted. | Do not punish the repetitive actions; accommodate the student's need for order while referring them to a trained psychological professional. |
| **Depression** | Prolonged sadness, sudden drop in academic performance, withdrawal from friends, and loss of interest in previously enjoyed play. | Offer a warm, non-demanding presence, monitor for isolation, communicate with parents, and refer for professional counseling. |
| **Suicidal tendency** | Expressing feelings of worthlessness, giving away prized possessions, or talking about wanting to disappear. | Recognize these urgent warning signs and immediately escalate the situation to a school counselor, principal, or mental health professional. |
| **Substance abuse disorder** | Smelling of alcohol or tobacco, sudden dramatic shifts in peer groups, bloodshot eyes, or lethargy during morning classes. | Document specific behavioral changes, avoid direct confrontation while under the influence, and involve school leadership and parents immediately. |
| **Anti-social behaviour** | Vandalizing school property, aggressive bullying, persistent rule-breaking, and a noticeable lack of empathy for others' pain. | Enforce clear, consistent boundaries, protect other students, and work with specialized counselors to address the underlying hostility. |

*Why this matters for a teacher:* Teachers are the first line of defense; knowing how to observe and respond to these behaviors ensures that students receive timely help rather than mere punishment.

## Key Terms

- **Adjustment** — The process of finding and maintaining a harmonious balance between one's internal needs and external environmental demands.
- **Maladjustment** — A state of psychological disharmony where an individual fails to cope with environmental demands, resulting in problematic behavior.
- **Defense Mechanism** — Unconscious psychological strategies used to cope with reality and maintain self-image when facing stress or failure.
- **Truancy** — The action of a student intentionally staying away from school without a valid reason or permission.

## Quick Recap

- Good adjustment leads to a balanced, productive life and is achieved through direct problem-solving or indirect defense mechanisms.
- Maladjustment is caused by a mix of personal, family, and school-related stressors, leading to behavioral issues.
- Prevention relies on a democratic, supportive school environment that meets a child's basic psychological needs.
- Teachers must learn to recognize the specific warning signs of various maladjusted behaviors—from timidity to severe anxiety—and respond with empathy, appropriate classroom management, and timely professional referrals.
