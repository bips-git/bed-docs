# Unit IV. Gender Issues in Curriculum

::: info Course

:::

## Curriculum and the Gender Question

The curriculum is the total learning experience provided by a school, including syllabi, activities, and school rules. Many people mistakenly believe the curriculum is gender-neutral. However, the "gender question" asks us to critically examine who designs the curriculum, whose knowledge is valued, and how it impacts boys and girls differently. Historically, school curricula were designed with a patriarchal bias, subtly preparing boys for public, professional life and girls for private, domestic life. A curriculum is only truly equitable when it actively challenges these traditional power structures instead of quietly reproducing them.

::: tip Example
In some schools, physical education periods force boys to play competitive sports like football or cricket on the main field, while girls are given skipping ropes or badminton rackets in a corner. This curriculum structure silently teaches students that male bodies are built for aggressive competition, while female bodies should engage in gentle recreation.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Teachers need to understand that the official curriculum is not always neutral, so they can identify and correct biases in the materials they are required to teach.

## Gender Issues in the NCF 2005 Framework: An Analysis

The National Curriculum Framework (NCF) 2005 made a massive leap forward in addressing gender in education. Through its specific "Position Paper on Gender Issues in Education," it shifted the paradigm by declaring that gender is a "people's issue," not just a "women's issue". The framework analyzed how rigid gender norms harm all students. It pointed out that while girls face social control and discrimination, boys also suffer heavily under patriarchy. Boys are often discouraged from being gentle or emotional and are forced into the stressful roles of breadwinners and protectors. NCF 2005 recommended that gender equity should not be a separate subject, but a central theme that runs through all school activities and subjects.

::: tip Example
Following NCF 2005 guidelines, instead of creating a separate "Women's Empowerment" chapter at the very end of a social studies book, curriculum designers weave stories of female leaders, scientists, and farmers directly into the main history and economics chapters to normalize their presence.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Teachers must apply the NCF 2005 philosophy by creating a classroom environment that frees both girls and boys from the pressure of traditional masculine and feminine expectations.

## Gender and the Hidden Curriculum

The "hidden curriculum" refers to the unwritten, unofficial, and often unintended lessons, values, and social rules that students learn at school. It operates silently alongside the formal academic syllabus. The hidden curriculum is transferred through a school's culture, daily routines, disciplinary methods, and the unconscious behaviors of the teachers. Very often, the hidden curriculum heavily reinforces traditional gender norms without anyone saying a word. 

::: tip Example
If a teacher routinely asks tall boys to move heavy wooden desks and asks girls to neatly decorate the bulletin board, the teacher is enacting a gendered hidden curriculum. Even if the formal textbook teaches equality, the students' actual takeaway is that physical labor belongs to men and decorative, domestic tasks belong to women.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Teachers must become hyper-aware of their unconscious habits, as students learn just as much from a teacher's actions as they do from a textbook.

## Gender in Text and Context (Textbooks) and Inter-sectionality with Other Disciplines

Textbooks are the primary tools used in Indian classrooms, making their "text and context" highly influential. When studying textbooks, we must look at gender's inter-sectionality with other disciplines. This means examining how gender biases bleed into subjects like Mathematics, Science, and Languages, rather than just Social Studies. Furthermore, gender in textbooks often intersects with other social categories like caste and class, usually presenting a narrow view of society (e.g., featuring only upper-middle-class, urban, male protagonists). 

::: tip Example
In a Mathematics textbook, a word problem might read: "Rahul invested ₹10,000 in the stock market, while Sita bought ₹500 worth of groceries." In a Science textbook, human anatomy diagrams might default to a male body. In History, pages are filled with male kings and soldiers, largely ignoring the contributions of queens and working-class women. This shows how gender bias cuts across multiple disciplines.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** A teacher must learn to critically read textbooks across all subjects so they can actively point out and correct these overlapping biases during their lessons.

## The Teacher as an Agent of Change

An "agent of change" is someone who actively works to transform harmful social structures rather than passively accepting them. In the education system, the teacher is the ultimate filter between the curriculum and the student. Even if a textbook is highly biased or a school's hidden curriculum is patriarchal, the teacher has the power to disrupt these patterns in their own classroom. Through inclusive lesson planning, equitable classroom management, and open dialogue, a teacher can reconstruct students' understanding of gender.

::: tip Example
A teacher realizes the prescribed science textbook features zero female scientists. Acting as an agent of change, the teacher prints out articles about the women scientists at ISRO who worked on the Chandrayaan mission, distributes them to the class, and leads a discussion on why women are historically underrepresented in STEM fields.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** It empowers educators to realize they are not just mechanical deliverers of a syllabus, but active builders of a fairer, more equal society.

## Key Terms

- **Curriculum:** The overall learning experience, including academic subjects, rules, and school culture provided to a student.
- **Hidden Curriculum:** The unspoken and unwritten social rules, values, and expectations that students absorb during their schooling.
- **Inter-sectionality across Disciplines:** The method of examining how an issue (like gender bias) appears across different academic subjects like math, science, and history.
- **Agent of Change:** A person who actively challenges the status quo and introduces progressive, equitable practices in their environment.

## Quick Recap

- Curricula are rarely gender-neutral; they often carry historical biases that favor traditional gender roles.
- NCF 2005 (Position Paper on Gender) stresses that gender is a people's issue and that rigid expectations harm both boys and girls.
- The hidden curriculum silently teaches gender norms through seating arrangements, chores, and teacher behaviors.
- Gender bias intersects with all disciplines; textbooks frequently stereotype men as economic actors/leaders and women as consumers/caregivers.
- Teachers have the ultimate power to act as agents of change by supplementing biased curriculum with inclusive, real-world examples.
