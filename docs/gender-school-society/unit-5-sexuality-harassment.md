# Unit V. Gender, Sexuality, Sexual Harassment and Abuse

## Development of Sexuality in Children: Key Influences

From a developmental-psychology perspective, sexuality is a natural, lifelong process that encompasses physical, emotional, and cognitive growth. In children and adolescents, this development is not merely biological but is heavily shaped by social environments. Three primary factors influence this journey. First, **gender conditioning** dictates how society expects a child to express themselves based on their assigned sex. Second, **body image** becomes critical during puberty; a child's self-esteem is deeply tied to how they perceive their changing physical form compared to societal ideals. Finally, **role models**—including parents, teachers, and peers—provide behavioral scripts. Children constantly observe these figures to understand what constitutes healthy boundaries, affection, and respect in relationships. 

::: tip Example
A middle school student begins slouching, wearing oversized clothes, and avoiding physical education classes. The teacher recognizes this as a developmental challenge related to body image during puberty, exacerbated by unrealistic media standards. Instead of forcing participation, the teacher coordinates with the school counselor to deliver an age-appropriate health module on natural physiological changes and body positivity.
:::

## Why Addressing Sexual Harassment Matters Across Settings

Sexual harassment is an assertion of unequal power that compromises a child's physical safety and psychological well-being. It is a systemic issue that occurs across various domains. In the **family**, the taboo surrounding domestic abuse or incest often forces victims into silence, making early detection by outside professionals crucial. In the **neighbourhood**, hostile public spaces (such as areas where street harassment or "eve-teasing" is common) restrict a student's mobility and access to education. Within **formal and informal institutions** (like schools, tuition centers, or sports clubs), unchecked harassment creates a toxic environment that disrupts learning, triggers anxiety, and causes school dropout. Addressing this comprehensively removes the culture of silence and impunity.

::: tip Example
A female student's grades suddenly drop, and she frequently arrives late or asks to go home early. A perceptive teacher investigates and discovers the student is facing daily verbal harassment on her walking route to school (a neighbourhood setting). The school administration intervenes by arranging a safe transport group and notifying local community policing, thereby restoring the student's safe access to education.
:::

## Agencies That Can Perpetuate Violence

Violence and harassment are often enabled by the very structures meant to protect individuals. The **family** can perpetuate violence when patriarchal norms normalize domestic abuse or prioritize male authority over a child's bodily autonomy. The **school** can become a site of violence through peer bullying, corporal punishment, or a lack of supervision in isolated areas. The **workplace** often enables harassment through rigid hierarchies, unequal power dynamics, and the absence of safe grievance mechanisms. Finally, **media (print and electronic)** plays a profound role by frequently objectifying bodies, normalizing aggressive behavior in movies or video games, and providing platforms for cyberbullying.

::: tip Example
A popular regional movie (electronic media) portrays a male protagonist repeatedly following and harassing a woman until she agrees to a date. A teenage student mimics this by continuously messaging a female classmate who has asked him to stop. The teacher uses this incident to conduct a clinical, non-judgmental classroom discussion on the legal definitions of consent and stalking, actively countering the media's harmful narrative.
:::

## Institutions and Mechanisms for Redressal

Educators are legally and ethically bound to act as frontline child safeguarders. The Indian legal framework provides specific tools for this. The **Vishaka Guidelines (1997)** were the first Supreme Court directives to address workplace harassment, later formalized into the **POSH Act (2013)** (Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act). The POSH Act mandates an Internal Complaints Committee (ICC) in every school to protect female staff and older students. For minors, the **POCSO Act (2012)** (Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act) is paramount. It is a gender-neutral law that makes reporting of child sexual abuse mandatory for professionals, including teachers. Failing to report suspected abuse is a punishable offense. Teachers must utilize institutional reporting mechanisms like the school's designated Child Protection Officer, the national toll-free **Childline (1098)**, and the **POCSO e-Box** (an online complaint management system run by the NCPCR).

::: tip Example
A child discloses an inappropriate touch by a distant relative to their class teacher. Following the mandate of the POCSO Act, the teacher does not attempt to investigate the claim personally or contact the family to resolve it "quietly." Instead, the teacher immediately reports the disclosure to the school's principal and designated child protection officer, who then officially notify Childline 1098 and local authorities to initiate clinical and legal support.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Body Image:** A person's subjective perception of their own physical appearance, which heavily influences their self-esteem during adolescence.
- **POSH Act (2013):** A law designed to prevent and redress sexual harassment of women at the workplace, mandating an Internal Complaints Committee (ICC).
- **POCSO Act (2012):** A comprehensive, gender-neutral law to protect children under 18 from sexual offenses, which enforces mandatory reporting by aware adults.
- **Mandatory Reporting:** The legal obligation of professionals (like teachers) to immediately report any suspicion or disclosure of child abuse to the appropriate authorities.

## Quick Recap

- Sexuality development in children is shaped by gender expectations, body image changes during puberty, and the behavioral scripts of role models.
- Harassment must be tackled across the family, neighbourhood, and schools to prevent trauma and ensure uninterrupted access to education.
- Violence is often structurally perpetuated by patriarchal families, unsupervised school environments, unequal workplaces, and objectifying media.
- The POSH Act (2013) protects women in the workplace (including schools), while the POCSO Act (2012) protects children and mandates immediate, formal reporting by teachers via mechanisms like Childline 1098.
