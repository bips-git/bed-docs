# Unit III. Gender, Power and Education

::: info Course
Course-VI (1.4.6) — Gender, School and Society
:::

## Gender Identities and Socialisation in the Family

Socialisation is the lifelong process through which individuals learn the norms, values, and behaviors expected of them by society. The family is the primary agent of socialisation. From the moment a child is born, the family begins to shape their gender identity. This happens through the clothes they are dressed in, the toys they are given, and the chores they are assigned. Families unconsciously teach children who holds power and what roles they are expected to play in adulthood. Boys are often taught to be strong, independent, and emotionally reserved, while girls are taught to be nurturing, accommodating, and focused on domestic duties.

::: tip Example
In a typical household, if guests arrive, a teenage daughter is immediately expected to go to the kitchen to help make tea and serve snacks. Meanwhile, her teenage brother is allowed to sit in the living room and join the adults' conversation. This silently teaches both children that a woman's domain is service, while a man's domain is social participation.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Understanding a child's family conditioning helps a teacher realize why a student might hesitate to speak up or participate in certain activities, allowing the teacher to gently and patiently encourage them to break those boundaries.

## Gender Identities and Socialisation in Schools

Schools act as the secondary agent of socialisation. While schools have a formal academic curriculum, they also have a "hidden curriculum." The hidden curriculum consists of the unwritten rules, teacher expectations, and subtle biases that pass on gender norms to students. Textbooks, seating arrangements, playground rules, and disciplinary actions all contribute to this. Schools can either reinforce the unequal power dynamics learned at home or actively challenge them by treating all students as equals capable of the same achievements.

::: tip Example
A teacher needs help reorganizing the classroom. They ask two tall boys to move the heavy wooden desks, and ask two girls to wipe the blackboard and decorate the bulletin board. Even without meaning to, the teacher is reinforcing the stereotype that boys are meant for physical labor and girls are meant for aesthetic, delicate tasks. 
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Teachers must constantly reflect on their own hidden biases so they do not unconsciously enforce limiting stereotypes through their everyday classroom management.

## Gender Identities and Socialisation in Other Formal and Informal Organizations

Beyond the home and the classroom, a child's gender identity is heavily influenced by the wider community. This happens through both informal organizations (like peer groups, local neighborhood clubs, and mass media) and formal organizations (like religious institutions, workplaces, and the legal system). These organizations often reflect the patriarchal power structures of society. Peer groups enforce strict rules about what is considered "cool" or acceptable for boys and girls. Mass media bombards youth with idealized images of masculinity and femininity. Formal organizations often reserve leadership and decision-making roles exclusively for men, reinforcing the idea that men are natural leaders.

::: tip Example
**Informal:** On a neighborhood playground, boys monopolize the central field to play a loud, expansive game of cricket, while girls are relegated to the edges to chat or play smaller games. 
**Formal:** In many local religious institutions, only men are allowed to lead prayers or perform major rituals, sending a clear message to young observers about who holds spiritual authority.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** A teacher must be aware of these powerful external influences to help students develop critical thinking skills, enabling them to question media and community messages rather than passively accepting them.

## Schooling of Girls and Women's Empowerment

Empowerment is the process of gaining the knowledge, confidence, and power to make independent life choices and challenge inequality. For girls and women, formal schooling is the most critical tool for empowerment. Education shifts the power balance. It provides girls with literacy, which leads to higher education and eventual economic independence. Economically independent women can delay early marriage, demand better healthcare, and participate in political and household decision-making. Schooling transforms a girl from a passive recipient of society's rules into an active citizen who can advocate for her own rights.

::: tip Example
A girl from a rural village completes her secondary education and learns about her legal rights and financial literacy. Because of her schooling, she confidently opens her own bank account and secures a job in a nearby town, allowing her to refuse a forced early marriage that her relatives were planning.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** A teacher is not just delivering a syllabus; they are actively building the foundation for a girl's future independence, safety, and voice in society.

## Key Terms

- **Socialisation:** The process by which individuals learn and internalize the customs, rules, and behaviors of their society.
- **Hidden Curriculum:** The unspoken, unwritten values and expectations that students learn in school alongside the formal academic lessons.
- **Peer Group:** A social group consisting of people who are similar in age, status, and interests, which heavily influences a person's behavior.
- **Empowerment:** The process of becoming stronger and more confident, especially in controlling one's life and claiming one's rights.

## Quick Recap

- Families introduce gender roles early by assigning different toys, chores, and expectations to boys and girls.
- Schools reinforce gender identities through the "hidden curriculum," including teacher biases and classroom chores.
- Informal groups (peers, media) and formal organizations (religion, workplaces) consistently model societal power dynamics and gender hierarchies.
- The schooling of girls is a direct catalyst for women's empowerment, leading to economic independence, delayed marriage, and enhanced decision-making power.
