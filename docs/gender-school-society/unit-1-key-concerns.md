# Unit I. Gender issues: key concerns

::: info Course
Course-VI (1.4.6) — Gender, School and Society
:::

## Definition of Gender

Gender refers to the socially constructed roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities of girls, women, boys, men, and gender-diverse people. It dictates how people are expected to act, dress, and speak based on their assigned sex. Unlike physical traits, gender is learned through socialization. Families, schools, media, and culture all teach us what is considered "masculine" or "feminine." Because gender is created by society, its definition changes across different cultures and time periods. 

::: tip Example
In many traditional Indian households, young boys are encouraged to play outside and be aggressive, while young girls are taught to stay indoors, help in the kitchen, and be quiet. When a boy falls, he is told "boys don't cry," teaching him to suppress his emotions.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Understanding that gender is a learned behavior helps teachers realize that they have the power to unteach harmful roles in their classrooms.

## Difference Between Gender and Sex

While people often use the words interchangeably, "sex" and "gender" mean very different things in sociology and education. Sex is biological and physiological. It is determined by chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive anatomy at birth (male, female, or intersex). Gender is a sociological concept. It refers to the cultural expectations wrapped around those biological differences. Sex is universal and mostly fixed; gender is fluid and varies by society.

::: tip Example
Biology dictates that only females can give birth to a child—this is an issue of **sex**. However, the societal expectation that women must be the primary caregivers for children while men go out to earn money is an issue of **gender**.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Teachers must recognize this difference so they never confuse a child's biological abilities with arbitrary societal limitations.

## Social Construction of Gender

The social construction of gender is the process by which society builds and maintains gender roles. Children are not born knowing how to be "boys" or "girls." They learn this through continuous social conditioning. Institutions like the family, religion, and the education system act as agents of socialization. They reward children for conforming to expected gender roles and punish or mock them when they break these rules. 

::: tip Example
Look at early school textbooks. A common Indian classroom scenario involves reading a storybook where the mother is shown cooking in a sari, and the father is shown reading a newspaper or leaving for the office. By reading this daily, children mentally construct the idea that domestic work is solely a woman's job.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Teachers are active agents of socialization who can either reinforce traditional gender constructs or consciously break them by using inclusive teaching materials.

## Gender Including Transgender and Third Gender, Sex, Patriarchy

To fully understand gender, we must look beyond the male-female binary and understand the power structures that govern society. 
- **Transgender and Third Gender:** A transgender person's gender identity does not align with the sex they were assigned at birth. The term "third gender" refers to individuals who identify neither as male nor female. In 2014, the Supreme Court of India legally recognized transgender people as a "third gender," granting them fundamental rights. 
- **Patriarchy:** This is a social system where men hold primary power. In a patriarchal society, men dominate political leadership, moral authority, social privilege, and control of property. It inherently places women and transgender individuals in subordinate positions.

::: tip Example
When a school admission form only provides two checkboxes for "Male" and "Female," it completely erases the identity of a transgender student, making them feel invisible. Furthermore, when a school only calls the father to sign important fee documents instead of the mother, it actively practices patriarchy.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Teachers must actively dismantle patriarchal habits and create a safe, legally compliant, and emotionally supportive space for transgender students.

## Gender Bias, Gender Stereotyping, and Empowerment

These three concepts explain how gender disparities play out in real time and how we can solve them.
- **Gender Stereotyping:** This is a generalized, rigid belief about the characteristics or abilities of a specific gender. 
- **Gender Bias:** This is the unequal treatment or action taken based on those stereotypes. It can be conscious or unconscious.
- **Empowerment:** This is the process of building self-confidence, critical thinking, and decision-making power in marginalized genders, enabling them to challenge inequality and make their own life choices.

::: tip Example
A teacher believes that boys are naturally better at mathematics and physics, while girls are better at languages and art (this is a **stereotype**). Because of this belief, the teacher calls on boys more often during science class and encourages only girls to participate in the school choir (this is **bias**). To counter this, a school runs a special robotics workshop exclusively for girls to build their technical skills and confidence (this is **empowerment**).
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** Recognizing personal unconscious bias is the vital first step a teacher must take before they can effectively empower their students.

## Equity and Equality in Relation with Caste, Class, Religion, Ethnicity, Disability and Region

Equality means treating everyone exactly the same. Equity means treating people fairly based on their unique needs to reach an equal outcome. Gender cannot be looked at in isolation. A student's experience of gender is deeply shaped by intersectionality—how their gender overlaps with their caste, economic class, religion, ethnic region (like rural vs. urban), and physical or mental disabilities. A wealthy, upper-caste boy from a big city experiences school very differently than a poor, Dalit girl from a rural village with a physical disability. 

::: tip Example
If a school gives a free standard printed textbook to every student, that is **equality**. However, a visually impaired girl from a marginalized caste living in a remote village cannot read that printed book and might face heavy family pressure to drop out. Providing her with an audio-book, a braille text, and a monthly stipend so her family lets her study is **equity**.
:::

**Why this matters for a teacher:** A teacher must look beyond just gender to understand how multiple layers of social disadvantage affect a child's ability to learn and succeed.

## Key Terms

- **Gender:** The socially constructed roles and behaviors assigned to people based on their perceived sex.
- **Sex:** The biological and physiological traits that define male, female, and intersex bodies.
- **Patriarchy:** A system of society where men hold the dominant power and women/marginalized genders are largely excluded from it.
- **Gender Stereotype:** A rigid, oversimplified belief about what men and women can or should do.
- **Equity:** Providing different levels of support based on individual needs to achieve fair outcomes for all.

## Quick Recap

- Gender is a cultural and social construct, whereas sex is a biological reality.
- Society builds gender norms through institutions like family, media, and education.
- The 2014 NALSA judgement legally recognized the third gender in India, mandating their inclusion.
- Patriarchy is a power structure that privileges men and subordinates others.
- Stereotypes are beliefs; bias is the discriminatory action born from those beliefs.
- Empowerment gives marginalized groups the agency to make independent life choices.
- Equity recognizes that students face different intersecting barriers (caste, class, disability) and requires tailored support, not just equal treatment.
